Goodhart's Law: When Your Metric Stops Measuring What You Think It Does

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." A tiny economics idea from the 1970s that turns out to explain a huge amount of what goes wrong in data science and AI.

I ran into this one sideways, not through a course.I was listening to Data Skeptic's [MINI] Goodhart's Law episode, one of their short, conversational "mini" episodes — and the way they built it up stuck with me more than a textbook definition ever would have: start with the plain-English law, then just walk through example after example until it clicks.

The law itself

Charles Goodhart was a British economist, and back in the 1970s he was talking about monetary policy, not machine learning, when he wrote something that got boiled down to this one line:

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

That's it. That's the whole law. The unsettling part is how much it explains once you actually sit with it. A metric works fine as long as it's just observing something. The moment people start optimizing for the metric itself, rather than for the underlying thing it was supposed to represent, the metric and the reality it once tracked start to drift apart — sometimes quietly, sometimes catastrophically.

The examples that make it click

There are some classic examples called the Cobra effect and the Rat effect, not sure if these are true stories. In Vietnam, the government wanted to get rid of rats, so they offered a bounty for every dead rat. They didn't want to have people to bring dead rats in the official place so they asked people to chop off the tails and bring them for the bounty. Later the police noticed that not only there were tailless rats roaming around the city but people started breeding rats to chop off their tails to get the money. The Cobra effect, officials paid people for killing snakes so people started breeding snakes and as soon as they found out, they ended the program. And the people the released all the snakes.

SEO

Search engines rank pages using signals like keyword frequency, backlinks, and dwell time. The moment "rank #1 on Google" becomes the actual target, an entire industry appears whose job is gaming those specific signals — keyword-stuffed content, backlink farms, engineered dwell time — often at the direct expense of the thing the ranking was supposed to indicate in the first place: whether the page is actually useful.

Call centers

Measure support quality by "average call handling time," and you'd assume shorter is better — a proxy for efficiency. Make it the target, though, and agents learn to hang up early, rush unresolved issues, or transfer callers just to get them off their own line. Handling time keeps dropping. Customer satisfaction, the thing you actually cared about, does not.

...and machine learning

This is the one that made the whole thing click for me, because it's not a hypothetical — it's a pattern I can point to directly in ML:

  • Accuracy as a target on imbalanced data. A model that always predicts the majority class can hit 95%+ accuracy while being functionally useless — the exact failure mode behind why class imbalance actually matters, not just a textbook caveat.
  • Engagement metrics in recommendation systems. Optimize purely for watch-time or click-through, and the system learns that outrage, controversy, and borderline content reliably win — because "keeps you watching" and "genuinely useful to you" are correlated only up to a point, and the optimizer doesn't know or care where that point is.
  • Reward hacking in RL. An agent rewarded for "points scored" in a boat-racing game famously learned to spin in a small circle collecting power-up pickups forever, instead of finishing the race — because the proxy reward and the actual goal quietly stopped being the same thing.
  • Leaderboard-driven research. Once a benchmark becomes the thing a field optimizes against, progress on the benchmark number and progress on the real underlying capability can decouple — models that ace a specific test set without the improvement generalizing anywhere else.

So what do you actually do about it?

The podcast doesn't offer a magic fix, and honestly, I don't think there is one — Goodhart's Law isn't a bug you patch, it's a structural fact about what happens when a proxy meets an optimizer. But a few things genuinely help:

  • Track more than one metric. A single number is trivially easy to game; several metrics that would all have to move together for the "cheat" to actually work are much harder to fake at once.
  • Periodically re-check the metric against the thing it's a proxy for. Does "accuracy" still track "the model is actually useful" once the class balance shifts? Does "watch-time" still track "the user is glad they watched this"? Proxies decay — they're not a one-time validation.
  • Be suspicious of metrics that improve unusually fast. A number moving faster than the underlying reality plausibly could is one of the more reliable tells that something's being gamed rather than genuinely improved.

None of this is exotic advice. It's mostly just: remember the metric was always a stand-in, not the actual goal — and go check on that relationship every so often instead of assuming it holds forever.
